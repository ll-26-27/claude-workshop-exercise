What to notice

The three views are one state. Drag [S] and the green circles fill, the marker slides along the curve, and the point on the double-reciprocal plot moves — all from the same number.

The curve bends. At low [S] it looks like a straight line through the origin, which is why rate seems proportional to substrate. Keep dragging and it flattens: every active site is busy, and more substrate has nowhere to go.

Km is marked on the horizontal axis. It is a concentration, not a rate — the [S] that gets you halfway to Vmax.

Try this

  1. Set [S] to 5 µM and note the rate. Predict the rate at 10 µM before you move the slider. Now set it to 10. Were you right? Try the same prediction from 50 to 100 µM.
  2. Put [S] exactly at the Km value. What fraction of Vmax do you get? Change Km and repeat. Does the fraction ever change?
  3. Turn on the competitive inhibitor and raise [I]/Ki. Which intercept on the Lineweaver–Burk plot moves — and which stays pinned?
  4. Now switch to non-competitive. The other intercept moves. Say out loud what that means about whether more substrate can out-compete the inhibitor.
  5. With a competitive inhibitor at [I]/Ki = 3, push [S] to 100 µM. Compare the rate to the grey dashed uninhibited curve. Can substrate win? Repeat with non-competitive.

Model limitations

This is the simplest possible enzyme. It assumes one substrate, one active site, no cooperativity, and a steady state that is reached instantly — so there is no time axis here at all, only the rate once things have settled.

Real enzymes have temperature and pH optima, are often inhibited by their own product, and may show sigmoidal rather than hyperbolic curves when subunits talk to each other. Allosteric enzymes such as phosphofructokinase do not obey the curve on screen.

The green circles are a visualisation of the occupied fraction, not a physical simulation of binding. Real molecules bind and release constantly; what stays steady is the proportion bound at any instant.

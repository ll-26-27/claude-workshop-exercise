What to notice

The red curve peaks after the green one — always, for every parameter setting. Predators cannot increase until there is already a lot of prey to eat, and by the time there are many predators the prey are already crashing.

How far after is not fixed. The textbook answer, a quarter of a cycle, is the small-oscillation result: it holds when the populations stay near the equilibrium point. On the big boom-and-bust orbits the lag is shorter — nearer a fifth or a tenth of a cycle. The readout gives you the measured fraction, so check it rather than trusting the number you were told.

Nothing in this model has a calendar. There is no winter, no drought, no external driver. The oscillation is generated by the interaction itself, which is the whole point.

The phase portrait is the same run with time thrown away. A closed loop means the populations return to exactly where they started and repeat — the loop is travelled anticlockwise, and one lap is one cycle.

Try this

  1. Watch one full cycle and say, out loud, the four-stage story: prey rise → predators rise → prey crash → predators starve. Match each stage to a quarter of the loop in the phase portrait.
  2. Raise β (predation). Predict first: do the cycles get faster, taller, or both? Watch the measured period change.
  3. Start close to equilibrium (prey 55, predators 22) and read the lag fraction. Now start far away (prey 120, predators 3) and read it again. The quarter-cycle rule holds in one case and not the other — which, and can you say why?
  4. Set starting prey and predators to the equilibrium values shown under the phase portrait. What happens to the loop? What does that tell you about that point?
  5. Start far from equilibrium (prey 120, predators 3). Does the loop ever spiral inward toward the equilibrium? Real populations usually do — what is this model missing?
  6. Try to drive the prey extinct by pushing β to maximum and δ high. You cannot. Name the assumption that makes extinction impossible here.

Model limitations

This is classical Lotka–Volterra, and it is famously wrong in instructive ways. Prey grow without limit when predators are absent — there is no carrying capacity, no food supply of their own. Predators eat at a rate proportional to prey density with no upper bound, as though handling and digesting took no time.

The orbits are neutrally stable: a nudge moves the system to a different loop permanently, rather than returning it. Real predator–prey systems usually spiral in toward a stable cycle or point. Adding a carrying capacity or a saturating functional response fixes this, at the cost of a model you cannot solve by hand.

Populations here are continuous real numbers, so 0.3 of a lynx is possible and nothing ever goes extinct by bad luck. Any real small population can be wiped out by chance alone.

The quarter-cycle lag quoted in most textbooks comes from linearising these equations about the equilibrium point. That approximation is good for small wobbles and poor for the large cycles this simulation shows by default — a useful reminder that a remembered result usually carries an unstated condition.
